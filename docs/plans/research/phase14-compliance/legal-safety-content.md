## 1. CSAM reporting (18 U.S.C. 2258A and related)

**(a) Rule.** A "provider" that obtains *actual knowledge* of facts showing an apparent violation of the listed child-exploitation offenses must report to NCMEC's CyberTipline as soon as reasonably possible. No duty to monitor or scan. Reports to NCMEC are protected from civil/criminal claims. Failure to report (knowing and willful) is fined.

**(b) Citations / quotes** (https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/2258A):
- 2258A(a)(1): providers "shall, as soon as reasonably possible after obtaining actual knowledge" of (a)(2)(A) facts, give NCMEC contact information and make a report to the CyberTipline. For (a)(2)(B) (planned/imminent) facts a provider "may" do so.
- 2258A(a)(2)(A): apparent violation of 2251, 2251A, 2252, 2252A, 2252B, 2260 involving child pornography; section 1591 (where it involves a minor); or 2422(b). **The REPORT Act (Pub. L. 118-59, signed 2024-05-07) added 1591 (minor) and 2422(b)** (child sex trafficking and enticement) -- confirmed at https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PLAW-118publ59/html/PLAW-118publ59.htm (s.4(a)(1)).
- 2258A(b): report contents are at the provider's discretion, to the extent within its custody/control (identifying info, timestamps, geographic info, visual depictions, the complete communication).
- 2258A(f): nothing requires a provider to "monitor any user, subscriber, or customer" or to "affirmatively search, screen, or scan for facts or circumstances."
- 2258A(e) penalties (knowing and willful failure to report), as amended by REPORT Act: first violation up to $850,000 (provider with >=100,000,000 monthly active users) or $600,000 (fewer); subsequent: $1,000,000 / $850,000.
- 2258A(h): a completed report is treated as a request to preserve contents for **"1 year after the submission"** (REPORT Act s.3 changed 90 days to 1 year); providers must preserve reasonably accessible visual depictions/data/files giving context, "maintain the materials in a secure location and take appropriate steps to limit access"; (h)(5) providers may **voluntarily keep longer** than one year to reduce online child sexual exploitation; (h)(6) preservation must be consistent with the most recent NIST Cybersecurity Framework within one year of enactment (i.e. by 2025-05-07).
- 2258A(g)(4): a provider filing a report may share information, including visual depictions, only with specified law enforcement, NCMEC, or as needed to respond to legal process (paraphrase of fetched summary; exact text not quoted).
- Later amendment: Pub. L. 119-60, div. H, s.8202(i), 2025-12-18 (139 Stat. 1844), only added "and all supplemental data included in the report" to 2258A(c) (NCMEC forwarding). No new provider duty found.

**2258B limited liability** (https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/2258B): a civil claim or criminal charge against a provider "arising from the performance of the reporting or preservation responsibilities" may not be brought in any Federal or State court; exceptions: "intentional misconduct," "actual malice," "reckless disregard to a substantial risk of causing physical injury without legal justification," or acting for "a purpose unrelated to the performance of any responsibility." REPORT Act added 2258B(d) (NCMEC-contracted vendors; negligence is an exception for them) and (e) (minors depicted reporting their own images).

**2258E "provider"**: "the term 'provider' means an electronic communication service provider or remote computing service" (RCS per 18 U.S.C. 2711; ECS per 2510). Whether a small website qualifies: unable to verify from primary text beyond the definition; a site that lets users upload/store/share images and send messages/comments plausibly offers ECS and/or RCS to the public. Counsel to confirm. NCMEC's own (archived 2017) page said any US company providing ECS/RCS to the public may register.

**(c) Dates.** REPORT Act enacted 2024-05-07. NIST preservation compliance 2025-05-07. No injunction/vacatur found.

**(d) What a site like this must do.**
- Treat detection of an apparent violation (including the site's own scanner hits that a human or the system confirms) as "actual knowledge"; build a decision rule for when a hit becomes a report; file "as soon as reasonably possible".
- Preserve the reported content and context for 1 year after report in secure storage with access restriction and NIST-CSF-consistent controls; may keep longer. Do not delete quarantined evidence early. Quarantine design (who can access, encryption, audit log) must meet that bar.
- Wire the automated filing path (it is built but unwired): there is currently no way to discharge the duty on a hit. Policies (privacy policy/ToS) should disclose scanning and reporting to NCMEC/law enforcement.
- Staff/vendor handling: statute limits disclosure to NCMEC, designated law enforcement, or per legal process (2258A(g)(4)). I found **no statutory text addressing who inside a provider may view apparent CSAM, or the use of third-party cloud/vendor storage for a provider's own evidence**; the REPORT Act "vendor" provisions concern NCMEC's contractors (2258B(d)), not a provider's vendors. unable to verify: whether storing evidence with a general cloud host or sending suspect images to a third-party image-generation/moderation API is permitted; counsel/NCMEC ESP team would settle it.

**NCMEC registration / web form vs API.** An archived 2017 NCMEC page said a US ESP may register by emailing espteam@ncmec.org; registration URL cited by a third-party doc: esp.ncmec.org/registration; API access needs separate credentials from NCMEC (https://roostorg.github.io/coop/1.0/integrations/ncmec.html, https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20170118202100/http://www.missingkids.com/cybertipline). The public form is report.cybertip.org. `unable to verify: whether a registered small ESP may discharge the 2258A duty via the manual web form instead of the API, and whether an unregistered provider's public-form report counts; ask NCMEC ESP team (espteam@ncmec.org) / counsel.` The statute itself requires only "a report to the CyberTipline" and does not mandate API use.

**AI-generated/synthetic CSAM.**
- 18 U.S.C. 2256(8)(B): digital/computer-generated image "that is, or is indistinguishable from, that of a minor engaging in sexually explicit conduct"; (8)(C): visual depiction "created, adapted, or modified to appear that an identifiable minor is engaging in sexually explicit conduct" (https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/2256). "Indistinguishable" = "an ordinary person viewing the depiction would conclude that the depiction is of an actual minor" (2256(11)); excludes drawings/cartoons/paintings.
- Those are "child pornography" for 2252A, which is in the 2258A(a)(2)(A) list, so they are reportable. 18 U.S.C. 1466A (obscene visual representations, includes drawings/CG; "It is not a required element ... that the minor depicted actually exist"; https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/1466A) is **not in the 2258A(a)(2)(A) list as extracted** (list per fetched page: 2251, 2251A, 2252, 2252A, 2252B, 2260, 1591, 2422(b)). Whether to report 1466A-only material is therefore voluntary-permissive; confirm with counsel (also: the extraction was a summary of the Cornell page, re-read the statute).
- Case law: *United States v. Anderegg* (7th Cir., 2026-08-25) held 1466A(b) unconstitutional as applied to private in-home possession of obscene virtual CSAM (per press summaries; opinion copy at https://www.fire.org/sites/default/files/2026/08/Opinion%20-%20United%20States%20v.%20Anderegg.pdf, not read). Production/distribution counts untouched; narrow as-applied; rehearing/cert status unable to verify. It does not touch the platform's reporting duty, and it concerns possession, not distribution.
- NCMEC receives a GAI-flag on the CyberTipline form; no dedicated ESP guidance on AI CSAM found (https://www.missingkids.org/cybertiplinedata, https://ncmec.org/GAI).

**(e) Counsel questions.** (i) Is the site an ECS/RCS "provider"? (ii) What counts as "actual knowledge" for scanner-flagged but unreviewed items, and may staff view to confirm? ## 2. TAKE IT DOWN Act (Pub. L. 119-12; 47 U.S.C. 223a and 223(h))

**(a) Rule.** Covered platforms must run a process for individuals (no account required) to request removal of nonconsensual intimate visual depictions, including AI "digital forgeries", remove within 48 hours of a valid request, and make reasonable efforts to remove known identical copies. Separate federal crimes for publishing such depictions.

**(b) Quotes.**
- Pub. L. 119-12, s.3(a)(1)(A): "Not later than 1 year after the date of enactment of this Act" a covered platform shall establish a process (enacted 2025-05-19; https://www.congress.gov/119/plaws/publ12/PLAW-119publ12.htm; codified https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/47/223a).
- s.3(a)(1)(B) request must include a signature, information to locate the depiction, a good-faith statement that it is not consensual, contact info. s.3(a)(2) notice must be "easy to read and in plain language". s.3(a)(3): remove "not later than 48 hours after receiving such request" plus reasonable efforts to remove known identical copies.
- Covered platform (s.4(3)): a website/online service/app that "serves the public" and "primarily provides a forum for user-generated content" or regularly publishes/hosts nonconsensual intimate depictions (the Cornell summary reads slightly differently; re-read s.4 for exact wording and exclusions: broadband, email, mostly preselected content sites).
- Digital forgery, 47 U.S.C. 223(h)(1)(B): "any intimate visual depiction of an identifiable individual created through the use of software," "machine learning, artificial intelligence, or any other computer-generated or technological means" that "when viewed as a whole by a reasonable person, is indistinguishable" from an authentic depiction (https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/47/223). Criminal: publishing adult forgery without consent up to 2 years; minor forgery up to 3 years (223(h)(3)-(4)).
- Enforcement: s.3(b): failure to reasonably comply "shall be treated as a violation of a rule defining an unfair or a deceptive act or practice" under FTC Act s.18(a)(1)(B).

**(c) Dates / status.** Compliance date 2026-05-19, **already passed**. FTC press release "FTC Begins Enforcing the TAKE IT DOWN Act" (May 2026): https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/05/ftc-begins-enforcing-take-it-down-act ; warning letters to a dozen websites 2026-05-20: https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/05/ftc-sends-warning-letters-companies-about-compliance-take-it-down-act ; business guidance https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-take-it-down-act (covers AI forgeries; notice "clear and conspicuous", suggested on home page and wherever intimate content may appear; victims need not have an account; civil penalty "$53,088 per violation"; complaint portal TakeItDown.ftc.gov). Not enjoined or vacated: unable to verify beyond absence in search results. DOJ has brought first criminal cases (WilmerHale alert 2026-06-15: https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/client-alerts/20260615-the-take-it-down-act-goes-live; secondary).

**(d) Concretely for this site.** Assume covered (user-submitted facts, comments, personal AI image outputs shared publicly). Build: a public, linked, plain-language notice (footer + share pages) explaining the process; a request form/email usable without an account with the four required elements; internal SLA: removal inside 48 hours incl. duplicates (hash match of identical copies, including in generated-image caches and share links); log of requests/outcomes; reject/handle bad-faith requests without delaying inside the clock. Because the product generates images of real people, the "digital forgery" angle is core: an intimate generated image of a real person is both a possible crime and a takedown trigger. The site should also block intimate/nude generations of real persons at the pipeline.

**(e) Counsel questions.** Is the site a "covered platform" given 13+/18+ gating, paid membership, and that most content is generated per user rather than hosted forum content? Is a platform-generated image "published" by the platform? FTC says guidance is not exhaustive and open scope questions remain for age/geography/paid-gated platforms (per search summary; unable to verify from FTC text). Does the statute give a good-faith over-removal safe harbor (the FTC page does not mention one; the statute text should be read, s.3(a) ... unable to verify). How to handle fraudulent takedown abuse.

## 3. DMCA safe harbor (17 U.S.C. 512; 37 C.F.R. 201.38)

**(a)** Hosting providers avoid monetary liability for user-posted infringing material if they meet 512(c)/(i) conditions, including a registered agent.

**(b)/(d) Conditions and mechanics** (https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/512):
- 512(c)(1): no "actual knowledge ... that the material ... is infringing" (and no red-flag awareness), no "financial benefit directly attributable to the infringing activity" where it can control it, and "responds expeditiously to remove, or disable access to, the material" after a notice.
- 512(c)(2): "designate an agent to receive notifications of claimed infringement" and make contact information available on the service.
- 512(c)(3)(A): notice elements incl. signature, identification of work and material, contact info, good-faith statement, and statement of accuracy "under penalty of perjury".
- 512(g)(2)(C): after a valid counter-notice restore "not less than 10, nor more than 14, business days following receipt of the counter notice" unless the claimant files suit; 512(g)(3) counter-notice elements incl. perjury statement and consent to jurisdiction; 512(f) liability for knowing misrepresentation.
- 512(i)(1)(A): adopt, reasonably implement and "inform subscribers and account holders" of a repeat-infringer termination policy.
- 37 C.F.R. 201.38 (https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/37/201.38): designation made electronically through the Copyright Office online system only; must post on the site: full legal name and street address of the service provider, alternate names the public might use, agent's name (and organization), agent's physical mail address, phone, email; **renew every three years** (designation "will expire and become invalid three years after it is registered"). Fee per Copyright Office FAQ (https://www.copyright.gov/dmca-directory/faq.html): **$6** to designate, amend or resubmit; renewal by amending/resubmitting starts a new 3-year period; reminder emails 90/60/30 days and 1 week before expiry. Directory page confirms the provider must "make certain contact information for the agent available to the public on its website" (https://www.copyright.gov/dmca-directory/).

**(c) Current-year developments.** *Cox Communications v. Sony Music Entertainment*, No. 24-171 (U.S. Mar. 25, 2026) reversed the $1B contributory-liability verdict, narrowing contributory infringement for ISPs and treating the DMCA safe harbors as defences rather than liability sources; commentators say a deficient repeat-infringer policy cannot alone create liability (summaries: https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/LSB11416.html, https://www.goodwinlaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/03/alerts-practices-supreme-court-scales-back-copyright; opinion itself not read). Safe harbor remains worth having. No enjoined/vacated item found.

**(d) Compliant DMCA page** should contain: agent contact block exactly matching the Copyright Office record; instructions and the 512(c)(3) elements for notices; counter-notice instructions and the 10-14 business-day restoration statement; repeat-infringer policy (strikes/termination, applied to accounts); statement that misrepresentation is actionable under 512(f); registered before launch (a designation is only effective once registered). Note site's own AI outputs: infringement claims about generated images based on user-supplied copyrighted photos/characters.

**(e) Counsel.** Does safe harbor reach platform-generated images (not "stored at the direction of a user")? Repeat-infringer policy design for a credit/membership product; treatment of third-party photo uploads that are copyrighted (the photographer's rights in a friend's photo).

## 4. Section 230 (47 U.S.C. 230)

**(a) Rule.** Platform is not treated as publisher/speaker of "information provided by another information content provider" (230(c)(1)); good-faith moderation protected (230(c)(2)(A)).

**(b) Text** (https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/47/230): (c)(1) "No provider or user of an interactive computer service shall be treated as the publisher or speaker of any information provided by another information content provider." (e)(1) no effect on federal criminal law incl. "chapter 110 (relating to sexual exploitation of children) of title 18"; (e)(2) "Nothing in this section shall be construed to limit or expand any law pertaining to intellectual property"; (e)(3) States may enforce laws "consistent with this section", inconsistent laws preempted; (e)(5) FOSTA carve-out for 18 U.S.C. 1595 civil claims/state prosecutions tied to 1591. (f)(3) an information content provider is any person "responsible, in whole or in part, for the creation or development of information."

**(c) Status.** User-submitted facts and comments: classic 230 territory (but not for IP or federal crime claims; and 230(c)(1) is not a defence to the site's own conduct such as failing a TAKE IT DOWN or federal reporting duty). Platform's own AI output: no appellate authority. Key 2026 decision: *Bouck v. Meta* (N.D. Cal., 2026-03-24, pleading stage) declined to apply 230 to ads that Meta's generative AI tools helped create; later dismissed on other grounds (June 2026) (secondary: Goldman blog https://blog.ericgoldman.org/archives/2026/06/section-230-doesnt-apply-to-generative-ai-enhancements-to-ad-copy-but-the-plaintiffs-lose-anyway-bouck-and-suddeth-v-meta.htm -- 403 on fetch, relayed from search summary; also https://natlawreview.com/article/bouck-could-make-it-easier-sue-tech-companies-provide-generative-ai-tools). CRS notes courts have not decided whether 230 covers generative AI output (https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/LSB11097; older). No statute enacted on this point found.

**(d) Practical.** Assume 230 may not protect the image the pipeline generates (the site "develops" it); the user supplies the prompt/photo, the site's pipeline plus the third-party model supply the content. Protection for the typed fact text and comments is stronger. Build moderation and policies as if the generated image is the site's own speech.

**(e) Counsel.** Characterisation of fact-template personalisation (site-authored or community-authored, tokenised with a user's name) under 230; whether the generator vendor's terms shift liability; insurance (media liability).

## 6. Defamation / false light for hyperbolic humor

Statements that cannot "reasonably be interpreted as stating actual facts" are not actionable. *Hustler Magazine v. Falwell*, 485 U.S. 46 (1988): public figures may not recover for IIED from a parody without "a false statement of fact which was made with 'actual malice'" (https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/485/46, read). *Milkovich v. Lorain Journal Co.*, 497 U.S. 1 (1990): no separate opinion privilege, but statements that cannot reasonably be interpreted as stating actual facts, rhetorical hyperbole and imaginative expression, are protected (Cornell URL returned 500; holding from memory, unable to verify quote: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/497/1/). Obviously absurd "facts" about a named person (superhuman boasts) fit this; risk rises when a "fact" is plausible-sounding, accuses of a crime/disease/sexual conduct, or the target is a private person who is harmed (false light and publicity statutes). A Terms/guidelines clause typically: users must not post content that states or implies false factual claims about a real person, no content that is harassing, sexual, or presents a private person in a way a reasonable reader would believe; the site may remove and a person can request removal; the site is not responsible for user-submitted content (and 230 for the text).

## 7. Terms and community guidelines mechanics

- **Assent.** Clickwrap (unchecked box or "I agree" button next to a link) is the safest; browsewrap fails without actual notice. 9th Cir. is strict on sign-in-wrap: *Chabolla v. ClassPass*, 129 F.4th 1147 (9th Cir. Feb. 27, 2025) (2-1, terms not binding; rehearing petition filed 2025-04-14, disposition unable to verify); *Godun v. JustAnswer*, 135 F.4th 699 (9th Cir. Apr. 15, 2025) (no conspicuous notice or unambiguous assent); a 9th Cir. memorandum dated 2026-11-05, No. 25-7161, applies Godun again (https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/memoranda/2026/11/05/25-7161.pdf, only listed in results, not read). Contra: 4th Cir. *Dhruva v. CuriosityStream* (Mar. 2025) enforced sign-in-wrap. Design implication: sign-up button should say that clicking means agreement, with adjacent conspicuous links to Terms and the arbitration clause. Secondary summaries: https://www.fenwick.com/insights/publications/ninth-circuit-reinforces-stricter-standards-for-online-user-agreements.
- **Changes.** Notice by email and banner, effective date, continued-use assent is weakest for arbitration changes; re-obtain clickwrap acceptance for material changes. (General practice; no specific 2026 authority retrieved.)
- **Arbitration/class waiver; mass arbitration.** AAA (Jan 2024) and JAMS (May 2024) mass-arbitration rules: single up-front fee instead of per-case fees (AAA: 25+ similar cases, claimant share $3,125, business $8,125; JAMS: 75+ demands, $7,500 initiation + 13% case-management fee) and batching/process arbitrators; apply only if the clause adopts them (secondary: https://www.troutman.com/insights/managing-mass-arbitration-risk-looking-beyond-provider-mass-arbitration-rules/). Typical clause features: informal dispute-resolution/notice period, bellwether/batching, opt-out window, small-claims carve-out, delegation clause. A 2024 9th Cir. decision (120 F.4th 670) found a mass-arbitration protocol unconscionable (secondary; unable to verify case name).
- **Minors.** 13-17 can disaffirm contracts, including arbitration agreements: *J.R. v. Electronic Arts* (Cal. Ct. App. 2024) allowed disaffirmance of an arbitration clause (https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/2024/e080414.html); delegation clauses can send disaffirmance questions to the arbitrator (*N.A. v. Nintendo*, N.D. Cal. 2023); continued use after disaffirmance can forfeit it (2026 Roblox-related rulings, secondary). So expect Terms against 13-17 to be voidable; keep the minors' account-level protections independent of the contract (COPPA is under 13 and out of scope here).
- **Auto-renewal/membership.** FTC click-to-cancel rule vacated by the 8th Cir. (July 2025, procedural); California ARL amendments (AB 2863) in force from 2025-07-01 (express affirmative consent, click-to-cancel, annual reminders) (secondary: https://btlaw.com/en/insights/alerts/2025/california-expands-automatic-renewal-law-new-requirements-now-in-effect).
- **Acceptable-use section should prohibit (for this product):** any sexual/nude/intimate content, any depiction of anyone under 18 in any sexual or suggestive context; uploading photos of anyone without that person's documented consent; uploading photos of minors; sexual, harassing, defamatory, or deceptive depictions of real people; impersonation passing generated images as real; hate/violence content; infringing content; circumventing safety filters or scanners; scraping/automated generation abuse; credit/payment fraud; content that violates sanctions/blocked geographies. Also: consent warranty by uploaders, licence grant to the site, takedown "image of me" route, the TAKE IT DOWN request channel, DMCA channel, repeat-violation termination, and notice that content is scanned and reports are made to NCMEC/law enforcement.
